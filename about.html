About me

Curious about complex systems. Serious about understanding them.

A fuller account of my background, research experience, working principles and the questions I want to pursue next.

I am an MSci Theoretical Physics student at the University of Birmingham, currently working at first-class standard. My main interest is in using mathematics, simulation and machine learning to study systems whose behaviour is too complicated to understand from equations alone. I am especially drawn to problems that combine a clear theoretical structure with noisy data, nonlinear dynamics or demanding computation.

How my interest in computational research developed

My degree has given me a strong foundation in theoretical and mathematical physics, with particularly strong results in chaos, Hamiltonian mechanics and data science. These subjects have shaped the way I approach research. I like beginning with the mathematical structure of a problem, identifying the assumptions that make a model possible and then building a numerical experiment that reveals what the formal theory does not make immediately obvious.

Computing became important to me because it turns abstract physical ideas into objects that can be tested. A simulation can expose convergence problems, unexpected phases and sensitivity to initial conditions. A machine-learning model can reveal structure in a dataset, but it can also fail in subtle ways. I therefore try to treat code as part of the scientific argument rather than as a separate technical task.

Statistical physics and the Ising model

My first substantial university research experience was in the Theoretical Physics Group at the University of Birmingham. I developed a configurable Python simulation of the two-dimensional Ising model, representing a lattice of spins and defining its behaviour through a Hamiltonian. I implemented calculations for local and total energy, magnetisation and spin interactions, then used the Metropolis algorithm to evolve the lattice under different temperatures, initial configurations and Hamiltonian choices.

The public notebook on my GitHub profile presents this project as an evolving computational investigation rather than a static demonstration. The code evaluates energy and magnetisation over Monte Carlo steps, performs temperature-dependent calculations and creates an animated black-and-white lattice showing the evolution towards lower-energy configurations.

This project taught me several research habits that I still use: define the physical quantity carefully, check local calculations before trusting global behaviour, study convergence instead of reporting only a final state and use visualisation as a diagnostic rather than as decoration. It also showed me how rich macroscopic behaviour can emerge from a simple local rule.

Astronomy, signal processing and machine learning

I later joined the Sun, Stars and Exoplanets Group for a summer research internship focused on stellar power spectra. I developed and refined an autoencoder intended to denoise and reconstruct spectra, generated physically informed synthetic examples and processed Kepler observations for a large sample of red giants into reproducible training and validation datasets.

The scientific objective was more important than the architecture alone. I examined learning curves, reconstruction losses and residuals, compared the learned approach with signal-processing baselines and investigated whether the latent representation retained information related to the frequency of maximum oscillation power. This experience strengthened my interest in scientific machine learning: models should not merely achieve a lower loss, but should preserve the information that matters for the underlying science.

The placement also gave me experience working with a larger observational dataset, discussing results with researchers and communicating the work at the 2026 National Astronomy Meeting. More importantly, it taught me how simulation and observation can complement one another. Synthetic data provide control and known ground truth; real observations expose the assumptions that the simulation leaves out.

Quantitative finance and current independent research

My current independent project applies the same research mindset to derivatives pricing. I am investigating GPU-accelerated machine learning for calibration of the Heston stochastic-volatility model. I am beginning with transparent foundations - Black-Scholes valuation, implied volatility and Monte Carlo simulation - before treating a neural network as a surrogate for a more expensive numerical calibration procedure.

The questions I care about are not limited to whether a network is fast. I want to measure pricing accuracy across parameter space, determine whether model parameters can be recovered reliably, compare conventional and learned calibration methods, test behaviour outside the training distribution and quantify the circumstances in which acceleration comes at the cost of stability or interpretability.

This work is part of a broader interest in quantitative research. I am drawn to environments where probability, statistics, optimisation and scientific computing are used to form hypotheses and test them against difficult data. I am also interested in the discipline required to avoid look-ahead bias, separate model development from evaluation and distinguish a statistically interesting pattern from one that could survive implementation costs.

How I work

I try to begin every project with a clear question and the simplest useful baseline. I value reproducibility, explicit assumptions and tests that can reveal when a result is wrong. When a model performs well, I want to know which information the model is using. When a model performs badly, I want the failure to teach me something about the data, the implementation or the original hypothesis.

I also care about communication. Alongside research, I tutor Mathematics and Physics at GCSE and A-Level. Teaching has made me more patient and precise, and it has reinforced a principle that applies to both physics and quantitative work: if I cannot explain an idea clearly, I should examine whether I understand it deeply enough.

What I want to explore next

I intend to pursue a PhD after completing my MSci. I am currently exploring quantitative research, scientific machine learning, stochastic modelling, simulation-based inference, GPU computing and computational physics. The common thread is a desire to work on problems where mathematical depth, careful computation and empirical testing are all necessary.

In the nearer term, I am looking for research-intensive internships and collaborations that will challenge me to work with more complicated datasets, stronger validation standards and larger computational systems. I am particularly interested in opportunities where I can contribute as a physicist while continuing to develop as a statistician, programmer and researcher.

Outside academic and computational work, I enjoy swimming, badminton and film criticism. These interests give me time away from technical problems while still feeding my appreciation for practice, analysis and clear argument.
