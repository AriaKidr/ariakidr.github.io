Computational Physics and the Ising Model

My first university research experience was as a Summer Research Assistant in the Theoretical Physics Group at the University of Birmingham. During this placement, I worked under the supervision of Professor Mark Dennis and Dr Jonathan Watkins on the computational modelling of phase transitions.

The central focus of this work was the two-dimensional Ising model, a model from statistical mechanics that represents a material as a lattice of interacting spins. Each spin can occupy one of two states, and the collective behaviour of these spins provides a comparatively simple but powerful way to investigate magnetism, thermal fluctuations, ordered states and phase transitions.

I developed a Python program that calculates the Hamiltonian energy and magnetisation of the lattice. I then implemented the Metropolis algorithm to introduce probabilistic spin changes and simulate the evolution of the system at different temperatures. This allowed me to explore the relationship between microscopic spin interactions and macroscopic quantities such as energy and magnetisation.

I produced graphs showing how the energy and magnetisation evolved over successive simulation steps and how their behaviour changed across different temperatures. I also animated the lattice so that its evolution could be viewed directly, making it possible to observe ordered regions forming and changing as the simulation approached lower-energy configurations.

This project strengthened my understanding of statistical mechanics while teaching me how to convert mathematical definitions into working computational procedures. It gave me practical experience in numerical modelling, stochastic algorithms, data visualisation, debugging and interpreting simulation results. I documented the program with step-by-step comments and published the work on GitHub so that other readers could follow the structure and development of the model.

My Ising model project is available on my GitHub page.

Machine Learning and Stellar Data

I am broadening my research experience through a Summer Research Internship with the Sun, Stars and Exoplanets Group at the University of Birmingham. This work has introduced me to a different area of physics by combining astrophysics, scientific data analysis and machine learning.

During this project, I have been developing an autoencoder designed to denoise stellar spectra represented through frequency and power data. An autoencoder is a neural-network architecture that learns a compressed representation of its input before reconstructing it. By training the model to preserve meaningful structure while reducing unwanted noise, it can support the analysis of complex observational datasets.

I began by producing artificial data that could be used as a controlled learning environment. Working with synthetic examples allowed me to observe the behaviour of the network, understand how changes to its architecture and training process affected its output, and identify problems before applying the model to real observations.

I then progressed to data from the Kepler mission, working with observations involving thousands of red giant stars. This stage of the project has given me experience of moving from an idealised computational problem to a large real-world scientific dataset, where noise, variation and uncertainty form an important part of the research challenge.

The internship is developing my understanding of neural networks and their use in scientific research, while also strengthening my ability to prepare, inspect and interpret numerical data. It has shown me how methods from computer science can be adapted to investigate problems in astrophysics and has encouraged me to explore research areas that connect theoretical ideas with modern computational tools.

Future Research and PhD Ambitions

In the longer term, I am interested in pursuing a PhD in physics. I have not yet decided on one specific field, and I view the remaining years of my MSci degree as an opportunity to explore different areas in greater depth before making that decision.

My research experiences have so far exposed me to two distinct but connected directions. My work on the Ising model introduced me to statistical mechanics, phase transitions, stochastic simulation and computational theoretical physics. My work with stellar data has introduced me to astrophysics, machine learning and the analysis of large observational datasets. I have enjoyed both projects, especially the process of building computational methods that reveal patterns and physical behaviour within complex systems.

Because of this, I am currently open-minded about the precise subject of a future doctorate. Possible areas of interest include computational physics, statistical mechanics, theoretical astrophysics, stellar physics and the application of machine learning to physical data. I am also interested in discovering other fields in which mathematical modelling, numerical simulation and scientific computing play central roles.

I hope that a PhD would allow me to investigate a focused research question over an extended period, develop greater independence as a researcher and make an original contribution to physics. I am particularly attracted to the combination of deep theoretical understanding and practical computational investigation, as well as the collaborative nature of modern research.

Over the remainder of my degree, I want to deepen my mathematical and physical knowledge, gain further research experience, improve my programming and data-analysis skills, and learn more about the research groups and doctoral projects available across different areas of physics. Rather than choosing a PhD field prematurely, I want to make an informed decision based on the subjects and research methods that I find most intellectually rewarding.
